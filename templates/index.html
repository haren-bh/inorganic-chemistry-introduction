PHYSICS & HISTORICAL SIGNIFICANCE

Understanding Rutherford’s Gold Foil Experiment

How a beam of alpha particles and a sheet of gold leaf revealed the atomic nucleus, complementing J.J. Thomson’s 1897 cathode ray discovery of the electron.

01
APPARATUS & METHODOLOGY

The Experimental Setup

Between 1908 and 1910 at the University of Manchester, physicist Ernest Rutherford instructed his colleague Hans Geiger and undergraduate student Ernest Marsden to probe atomic structure by firing high-speed alpha particles at ultra-thin metal foils.

  • Radioactive Alpha (α) Source: A sample of radium or radon enclosed in a thick lead block shield emitted alpha particles (He2+ nuclei with charge +2e and mass 4 u) at speeds of ≈ 1.5 × 107 m/s (~5% the speed of light).
  • Lead Collimator Slit: A narrow aperture in a lead plate absorbed diverging radiation and collimated the escaping alpha particles into a tight, pencil-thin parallel beam.
  • Ultra-Thin Gold Leaf (Au Foil): Gold was chosen for its extraordinary malleability, allowing it to be beaten into a foil just 4 × 10−5 cm (≈ 400 nm) thick—roughly 1,000 atomic layers—so alpha particles could penetrate without being stopped.
  • Circular ZnS Scintillation Screen & Microscope: Surrounding the foil was a circular screen coated with zinc sulfide (ZnS). Whenever an alpha particle struck the screen, it produced a microscopic flash of green light (a scintillation), counted in a pitch-dark room through a rotatable microscope.
  • Evacuated Vacuum Chamber: The entire apparatus was sealed inside an air-free vacuum chamber so alpha particles would not collide with nitrogen or oxygen molecules before reaching the detector.
02
EXPERIMENTAL MEASUREMENTS

Experimental Results

Earlier in 1897, J.J. Thomson’s Cathode Ray Tube Experiment had proven that atoms contain lightweight, negatively charged electrons (e−). When Geiger and Marsden fired heavy, positively charged alpha particles at gold leaf, they recorded three landmark observations:

> 99%

1. Undeflected Transmission (θ ≈ 0°)

The vast majority of alpha particles passed straight through the gold leaf with virtually zero deflection. Conclusion: The atom is overwhelmingly composed of empty space.

~ 0.99%

2. Small- to Moderate-Angle Deflections (1° < θ < 90°)

A small fraction deflected by several degrees due to strong electrostatic (Coulomb) repulsion when passing near a concentrated positive charge center.

~ 1 in 8,000

3. Large-Angle Backscattering (θ > 90°, up to 180°)

To everyone’s astonishment, roughly 1 in 8,000 alpha particles rebounded backward toward the source. Only an encounter with an extraordinarily dense, massive, positively charged core could reverse a speeding He2+ projectile.

“It was quite the most incredible event that has ever happened to me in my life. It was almost as incredible as if you fired a 15-inch artillery shell at a piece of tissue paper and it came back and hit you.”
— Ernest Rutherford
03
PARADIGM SHIFT IN SCIENCE

How It Changed Physics

In May 1911, Rutherford published his landmark paper deriving the exact mathematical law of Coulomb scattering and establishing the Nuclear Structure of the Atom, transforming physics forever:

  • Discovery of the Atomic Nucleus: Rutherford proved that over 99.9% of an atom’s mass and all of its positive charge (+Ze) are packed into a central nucleus of radius ~10−14 m—roughly 10,000 to 100,000 times smaller than the diameter of the atom (~10−10 m).
  • Completing the Subatomic Picture (with Thomson’s 1897 CRT): Combined J.J. Thomson’s experimental discovery of the negative electron (1897) with Rutherford’s experimental discovery of the positive nucleus (1911) into a unified nuclear architecture.
  • Catalyst for Quantum Mechanics (Bohr & Schrödinger): Classical Maxwellian electromagnetism predicted that orbiting electrons would radiate energy continuously and spiral into Rutherford’s nucleus in ~10−11 seconds. Resolving this instability crisis led directly to Niels Bohr’s quantized orbitals (1913) and Erwin Schrödinger’s wave equation (1926).
  • Birth of Nuclear & Particle Physics: Pioneered scattering cross-section analysis (dσ/dΩ ∝ csc4(θ/2)) as the universal tool for probing invisible subatomic structure—directly inspiring the discovery of the proton (1919), the neutron (1932), and quarks via deep inelastic scattering (1968).
